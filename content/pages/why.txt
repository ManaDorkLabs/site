## Cooperativism as coordination vs capitalism as extraction

:::compare
### Coordination Costs
- Collective bargaining
- Meeting overhead
- Referendum expenses
- Preference alignment
- Conflict resolution
- Capital access
### Extraction Costs
- Management surveillance
- Compliance and tracking systems
- Lack of employee motivation due to limited ownership
- Investor relations overhead
- Misalignment between short and long term gain
- **Shareholder dividends**
:::

## The Thesis

Capitalism has outlived its usefulness. Despite driving innovation for the last two hundred years, it has caused wealth to become hyper concentrated in the hands of a tiny minority. This minority is then using that wealth to further increase their own power, at the expense of everyone else. Fortunately, cooperatives have the power to eliminate excessive wealth inequality (and therefore the imbalance of power) that capitalism has generated. By fairly distributing ownership amongst those who *produce* value (rather than amongst those who simply have capital), civilization can finally solve not just wealth inequality, but arguably every human-centric problem on the planet.

Because a standard corporation is legally required to maximize returns for its shareholders, the capitalist demand for extraction is not reducible; if the corporation can extract more value, *it is legally obligated to do so*. The often missed extrapolation here is that this shareholder profit comes at the expense of both the employees who work for the business and the customers who purchase from the business. **Shareholder profits represent a Dead Weight Loss tax on transactions between producers and consumers.** An equivalent business that does not pay this tax could both charge less *and* pay its employees more.

Cooperatives, on the other hand, are not built with the same incentives; the "owners" are not shareholders, but members: sellers, buyers, users, patrons, etc. The cooperative does not exist as a profit maximizing entity, but rather as a **cost minimization entity for everyone who utilizes it.**

The key comparison for evaluating efficiency then becomes:
> Are the coordination costs of running a cooperative less than the extraction costs of running a capitalist corporation?

Historically, the answer to this has been no. Coordinating votes, coming to consensus, and accessing capital are all activities that democratic cooperatives wrestle with more than an un-democratic privately-owned business. Crucially, however, the economic efficiency of the cooperative structure can be improved, and this improvement is just now becoming enabled by the explosive advancement of digital technology over the last two decades.

## The Asymmetric Cost Reducer

Blockchain technology (or distributed ledger technology, DLT) is an untapped resource for solving coordination problems. DLT, in its initial incarnation as "decentralized finance", rightfully earned a stigma that unfortunately still prevents many from evaluating its other use cases more rationally. Far beyond merely digitizing Ponzi schemes, DLT creates the potential for large communities to **achieve consensus more cheaply than ever before in human history**.

DLT's capacity to trustlessly facilitate voting, governance, data storage, provenance, escrow, payments, and countless other online activities will inevitably revolutionize every digital industry. Using marketplace cooperatives as the point of entry, DLT could power an economic revolution capable of upending the entrenched pro-capitalist order.

**First slowly, then all at once.**
